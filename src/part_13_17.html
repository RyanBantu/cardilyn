2.1

What is calcification?

Everything below traces where the calcium in "coronary calcium score" actually comes from — starting with cholesterol, ending with calcium deposited in artery walls, valves, or muscle.

Calcification is the body depositing calcium into tissue. In the heart, it comes in two biologically different forms:

  • Dystrophic calcification — calcium depositing into tissue that's already damaged, dying, or chronically inflamed, even though blood calcium levels are completely normal. This is the mechanism behind coronary artery calcification, valve calcification, and post-heart-attack scar calcification.
  • Metastatic calcification — calcium depositing into otherwise-healthy tissue because blood calcium or phosphate levels are abnormally high (seen in kidney failure or severe hormone disorders). Less common in the heart, but can compound the dystrophic process in people with chronic kidney disease.

Almost everything discussed in this section is the dystrophic type — the body reacting to damage, not to a calcium overload in the blood.

2.2

LDL cholesterol and the endothelium

LDL (low-density lipoprotein) is a particle that carries cholesterol from the liver to the rest of the body, where cells use it to build membranes and hormones. The endothelium is the single-cell-thick lining inside every blood vessel — normally smooth, selective, and resistant to blood cells sticking to it.

The trouble starts when the endothelium is already stressed — by high blood pressure, smoking, high blood sugar, or inflammation. At that damaged spot, LDL slips through into the vessel wall, gets chemically oxidized, and is recognized by the immune system as a threat. White blood cells rush in, gorge on the oxidized LDL, and become swollen "foam cells" — the earliest visible building block of plaque. This cycle feeds itself: more damage lets in more LDL, which causes more damage.

2.3

How LDL is produced, step by step

LDL isn't made directly — it's the end product of the liver's fat-export particle being progressively stripped down as it circulates.

Liver
assembles VLDL with apoB-100
VLDL
LPL + GPIHBP1 strip triglycerides
IDL
further triglyceride loss
LDL
binds LDL receptors on cells
Liver → VLDL → (LPL + GPIHBP1 at capillary walls) → IDL → LDL

Each step matters: VLDL carries the liver's exported triglycerides and cholesterol. LPL (lipoprotein lipase), anchored onto capillary walls by GPIHBP1, strips out triglycerides for tissues to burn or store — the same enzyme system that processes dietary fat from chylomicrons, described next. As VLDL loses triglycerides it becomes IDL, then finally LDL — small, dense, and cholesterol-rich.

2.4

Chylomicrons, triglycerides, and the fat transport system

  • Triglyceride — the body's main storage form of fat: one glycerol backbone with three fatty acid chains attached. Dense in energy, easy to store, easy to break back down when needed.
  • Chylomicron — the largest lipoprotein particle, built by intestinal cells right after a fatty meal to carry dietary triglycerides into the lymphatic system and then the bloodstream.
  • Lipoprotein lipase (LPL) — the enzyme that hydrolyzes triglycerides inside chylomicrons and VLDL into free fatty acids, letting nearby muscle and fat tissue absorb them.
  • GPIHBP1 — an anchor protein embedded in capillary walls that captures LPL (made inside muscle/fat cells) and holds it in place on the blood-facing surface, where it can actually reach passing lipoprotein particles.

Together, these four pieces explain both halves of fat transport: chylomicrons handle fat fresh from a meal, VLDL handles the liver's own recycled fat, and both are processed by the same LPL/GPIHBP1 machinery sitting on capillary walls.

2.5

Bile and the enterohepatic loop

Bile plays two roles: helping absorb fat, and acting as one of the body's only real export routes for excess cholesterol.

The recycling loop is efficient by default — which is exactly why it doesn't remove much cholesterol unless something (fiber, medication) blocks reabsorption.

Fiber and bile acid sequestrant medications work by blocking that reabsorption step: fewer bile acids return to the liver, so the liver pulls more cholesterol from the blood to make replacements — indirectly lowering LDL.
