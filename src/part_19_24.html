2.9

Diabetes and obesity's specific contribution

Obesity

  • Visceral fat secretes inflammatory adipokines, worsening endothelial injury directly.
  • Produces "atherogenic dyslipidemia" — higher triglycerides, lower HDL, and small dense LDL particles that oxidize more easily.
  • Drives insulin resistance, feeding directly into the diabetes mechanisms below.

Diabetes

  • Insulin resistance increases liver VLDL production, feeding more raw material into the LDL pathway.
  • Chronic high blood sugar forms AGEs (advanced glycation end-products), damaging the endothelium directly — separate from the LDL-driven injury pathway.
  • Glycated LDL is harder for LDL receptors to clear, so it lingers in the blood longer.
  • Diabetes can trigger medial calcification — vascular smooth muscle cells transforming into bone-like cells in the artery wall itself, a pathway partly independent of plaque.
2.10

How the buildup can be reduced

  • Reduce saturated/trans fat, increase soluble fiber, cut added sugar and refined carbs
  • Regular aerobic exercise — improves LPL activity, HDL, and insulin sensitivity together
  • Weight loss, especially visceral fat reduction, if overweight
  • Quit smoking, manage blood pressure, prioritize sleep and stress management
  • Medical options where appropriate: statins, bile acid sequestrants, ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors, and diabetes/weight-management medications with cardiovascular benefit

This is a conversation to have with a doctor for anything personalized — labs, other conditions, and medication interactions all matter for the right plan.

2.11

Three places calcification happens in the heart

Coral: artery wall (plaque-driven) · blue: aortic valve (wear-driven) · amber: muscle tissue (scar-driven)
  • Coronary artery calcification (CAC) — inside atherosclerotic plaque in the artery wall; what a calcium score measures.
  • Valvular calcification — on a valve leaflet, most often the aortic valve, from decades of mechanical stress plus lipid deposition; can progress to aortic stenosis.
  • Myocardial calcification — directly in heart muscle tissue, usually after a large heart attack leaves dead tissue that later calcifies as it scars.
