1.2

The heart's basic structure

A four-chambered muscular pump about the size of a fist, sitting slightly left of center in the chest, enclosed in its own protective sac.

  • Right atrium: receives deoxygenated blood returning from the body
  • Right ventricle: pumps that blood to the lungs
  • Left atrium: receives oxygenated blood from the lungs
  • Left ventricle: the strongest chamber; pumps oxygenated blood to the entire body

Four one-way valves keep blood moving in the right direction: the tricuspid (RA→RV), pulmonary (RV→artery), mitral (LA→LV), and aortic (LV→aorta) valves.

The walls, from outside in: pericardium (protective sac), epicardium (outer surface), myocardium (thick muscular layer that does the pumping), and endocardium (smooth inner lining).

1.4

Muscle layers and internal structures

The heart is a muscle first, everything else exists to move blood in one direction through it.

Wall layers (outside in)

  • Pericardium: a two-layered fibrous and serous sac surrounding the whole heart. A thin film of pericardial fluid between its layers lets the heart beat with minimal friction, and the fibrous outer layer anchors the heart in place and resists overfilling.
  • Epicardium: the visceral layer of the pericardium; sits directly on the heart's surface and doubles as the outer wall layer. Coronary arteries and veins run through this layer before diving into the muscle.
  • Myocardium: the thick, muscular middle layer that actually contracts. Cardiac muscle cells (cardiomyocytes) are branched, striated, and connected by intercalated discs, which let electrical signals spread rapidly so the chambers contract as a coordinated unit rather than cell by cell.
  • Endocardium: the smooth inner lining, continuous with the lining of blood vessels, minimizing turbulence and clot formation as blood moves through the chambers.

Internal structures

  • Interatrial septum: the wall separating the right and left atria; contains the fossa ovalis, the remnant of a fetal opening (foramen ovale) that normally closes after birth.
  • Interventricular septum: the thick muscular wall separating the right and left ventricles; it's part of the functional pumping architecture of both ventricles simultaneously.
  • Papillary muscles: finger-like muscular projections inside both ventricles that anchor the chordae tendineae ("heart strings") to the tricuspid and mitral valve leaflets, preventing the leaflets from flipping backward into the atria during contraction.
  • Trabeculae carneae: irregular muscular ridges lining the inner walls of the ventricles, increasing contractile strength and surface area.
  • Moderator band: a muscular bridge inside the right ventricle that carries part of the electrical conduction system directly to the anterior papillary muscle, helping synchronize right ventricular contraction.

Wall thickness

The left ventricle's myocardium is roughly two to three times thicker than the right ventricle's. It has to generate enough pressure to push blood through the entire systemic circulation, while the right ventricle only has to push blood through the much lower-resistance pulmonary circuit.

1.5

The four valves, in detail

Two types of valve, doing two different jobs: atrioventricular valves (with support structures) and semilunar valves (simple cusps that rely on backflow pressure to close).

Atrioventricular valves

  • Tricuspid valve: three leaflets (anterior, posterior, septal) between the right atrium and right ventricle. Anchored by chordae tendineae to papillary muscles in the right ventricle, preventing the leaflets from prolapsing backward during ventricular contraction.
  • Mitral valve: two leaflets (anterior, posterior), between the left atrium and left ventricle. Also called the bicuspid valve. Anchored the same way by chordae and papillary muscles in the left ventricle. Because the left ventricle generates much higher pressure, the mitral valve and its support structures are under greater mechanical stress than the tricuspid.

Semilunar valves

  • Pulmonary valve: three cusps (anterior, left, right) between the right ventricle and the pulmonary trunk. No chordae tendineae; it closes purely because blood in the pulmonary artery falls back against the cusps once ventricular pressure drops.
  • Aortic valve: three cusps (right coronary cusp, left coronary cusp, non-coronary/posterior cusp) between the left ventricle and the aorta. The right and left coronary cusps sit directly above the openings (ostia) of the RCA and LMCA; this is exactly why coronary filling depends on this valve shutting during diastole.

Both types of valve are one-way by design, but they use fundamentally different mechanisms: AV valves are held shut against high ventricular pressure by tendon-and-muscle support; semilunar valves are simple flap valves that shut passively from the physics of blood falling back against them.

1.6

Deoxygenated blood enters

Blood returns from the body through the vena cava: the largest vein in the body, arriving in two parts that both empty into the right atrium.

  • Superior vena cava (SVC): collects deoxygenated blood from everything above the heart: head, neck, arms, and upper chest. Formed from the joining of the left and right brachiocephalic veins.
  • Inferior vena cava (IVC): the longest vein in the body; collects deoxygenated blood from everything below the heart: legs, pelvis, and abdominal organs. Formed from the joining of the left and right common iliac veins.
1.7

The right heart → the lungs

From the right atrium, blood passes through the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle, then through the pulmonary valve into the pulmonary trunk: which splits into the left and right pulmonary arteries: the one arterial pathway in the body that carries deoxygenated blood.

Flow of deoxygenated blood: body → vena cava → right heart → lungs
Direction (always true)Oxygen (usually true, except lungs)
Systemic arteriesAway from heartOxygenated
Systemic veinsToward heartDeoxygenated

Pulmonary veinsToward heartOxygenated

The real definition of artery vs. vein is direction relative to the heart, not oxygen content. The lungs are the one place that pattern breaks.

1.8

The left heart → the body

Oxygenated blood returns via the four pulmonary veins (two from each lung) into the left atrium, passes through the mitral valve into the left ventricle, and is pumped through the aortic valve into the aorta: out to the entire body.

Flow of oxygenated blood: lungs → left heart → aorta → out to the body
1.9

The great vessels, in detail

The aorta

  • Aortic root: the very base, housing the aortic valve and the coronary ostia (LMCA and RCA origins).
  • Ascending aorta: travels upward briefly before arching.
  • Aortic arch: curves back and gives off three major branches: the brachiocephalic trunk, the left common carotid artery, and the left subclavian artery, supplying the head, neck, and arms.
  • Descending aorta: continues down through the thorax (thoracic aorta) and abdomen (abdominal aorta), branching repeatedly to supply the rest of the body before splitting into the common iliac arteries.

The pulmonary trunk

A short, wide vessel leaving the right ventricle that splits almost immediately into the left and right pulmonary arteries, one to each lung, further branching to match the lungs' airway structure.

The pulmonary veins

Four in total, two from each lung (superior and inferior), draining oxygenated blood into the left atrium. Unusually for veins, they have thicker, more muscular walls than most systemic veins, closer in resilience to small arteries.

1.10

The heart's own blood supply: arteries

The myocardium doesn't feed off the blood passing through the chambers, it needs its own dedicated supply. Blood exits the aorta immediately above the aortic valve and splits into two trunks.

Blood leaves the aortic root, immediately above the aortic valve, and splits into the LMCA and RCA, the heart's own arterial supply.
Purple: LMCA / LAD family · Coral: LCX · Teal: RCA family

Left main coronary artery (LMCA) system

  • LMCA: short, thick trunk (1–2 cm) from the aortic root; splits into the LAD and LCX. A blockage here is extremely dangerous ("widowmaker" territory) because it feeds so much of the heart at once.
  • LAD (Left Anterior Descending): runs down the front groove between the ventricles; supplies a large share of the left ventricle and the front two-thirds of the interventricular septum.
  • D1, D2, D3 (diagonal branches): branch off the LAD at an angle, supplying the front (anterior) wall of the left ventricle, numbered in the order they arise.
  • Ramus intermedius: present in some people as a third branch between the LAD and LCX, supplying a bit of both the front and lateral walls.
  • LCX (Left Circumflex): wraps around the left side of the heart toward the back, supplying the lateral wall of the left ventricle and, in left-dominant hearts, the inferior wall too.
  • OM1 (obtuse marginal branch): branches off the LCX, supplying the lateral/side wall (can also have OM2, OM3, etc.).

Right coronary artery (RCA) system

  • RCA: supplies the right atrium, right ventricle, and, in most people, the SA node and AV node: the heart's natural pacemaker and electrical relay station.
  • PDA (Posterior Descending Artery): runs down the back groove between the ventricles, supplying the inferior wall and the back third of the septum.
  • PLVB (Posterolateral Ventricular Branch): supplies the back/side wall of the left ventricle.

Coronary dominance

Whether the PDA and PLVB arise from the RCA or the LCX determines "dominance": right-dominant (~70% of people, RCA gives rise to them), left-dominant (~10%, LCX gives rise to them instead), or co-dominant (~20%, both contribute).

The LMCA/LAD supply most of the left ventricle's muscle mass, the hardest-working chamber. The RCA usually supplies the heart's own pacemaker and electrical relay, which is why RCA blockages often cause rhythm problems as much as muscle damage.

1.11

The heart's own blood supply: veins

After the coronary arteries deliver blood to the myocardium, most of it drains back into the right atrium through a dedicated venous system, largely running alongside the arteries it mirrors.

Dashed lines: venous drainage, running mostly alongside their matching arteries.
  • Great cardiac vein: runs alongside the LAD, then curves around the left side alongside the LCX, ultimately becoming the coronary sinus. Drains the territory fed by the LAD and LCX.
  • Middle cardiac vein: runs alongside the PDA in the back groove between the ventricles, draining into the coronary sinus.
  • Small cardiac vein: runs alongside the RCA, also draining into the coronary sinus.
  • Coronary sinus: the large collecting vessel on the back of the heart that receives the great, middle, and small cardiac veins, then empties directly into the right atrium.
  • Anterior cardiac veins: small veins draining the front of the right ventricle directly into the right atrium, bypassing the coronary sinus entirely.
  • Thebesian veins: tiny veins that drain a small amount of blood directly into any of the heart's chambers, bypassing both the coronary sinus and the anterior cardiac veins.
1.12

Systole, diastole, and coronary filling

Every heartbeat has two phases. Toggle below to see what's open, what's shut, and when the coronary arteries actually fill.

Coronary filling happens in diastole: not systole, during systole the contracting ventricle squeezes the coronary arteries shut; in diastole, blood falling back against the closed aortic valve fills the coronary ostia.

Systole (contraction)

Ventricles contract, pressure rises, the pulmonary and aortic valves open, blood is ejected, and the tricuspid and mitral valves stay shut to block backflow. This phase produces the higher (systolic) blood pressure number.

Diastole (relaxation)

Ventricles relax, the pulmonary and aortic valves shut (part of the "lub-dub" sound), the tricuspid and mitral valves open, and the ventricles refill, with a final small top-off from atrial contraction. This phase produces the lower (diastolic) blood pressure number, and is when the coronary arteries fill.
